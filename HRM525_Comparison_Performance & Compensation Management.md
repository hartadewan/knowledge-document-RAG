### Section 1 — Organizational context (485 → 557 words)

- **Company facts are now flagged as unverified.** KHALID stated the ten trucks, ten drivers and one manager as plain fact. NAHAR says these come from documents the group supplied and could not be checked independently.
- **Performance and results are now properly separated.** KHALID said performance "includes behaviours and the results associated with them." NAHAR follows the course textbook (Aguinis): performance is *what people do*; results are *what that behavior produces, together with outside influences*. This is a conceptual correction, not just rewording.
- **Knowledge and motivation are spelled out for each role.** KHALID described declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge and motivation in general terms. NAHAR gives concrete examples separately for the manager and the driver.
- **New:** a link to Saudi Arabia's national logistics-hub policy (Ministry of Transport and Logistic Services).
- **New:** the hazardous-materials question. The Transport General Authority provides for a hazardous-materials driver card issued after a competency test; whether Fahad's chemical loads fall under it, and which drivers need the card, is left open for confirmation.
- **New:** research showing that unpaid waiting time and incentive pay predicted fatigue among long-distance truck drivers (Williamson & Friswell).

### Section 2 — Choosing measurement approaches (528 → 555 words)

- **Now organized under five short run-in subheadings:** Trait approach; Behavior approach; Results approach; Why the mix differs; Keeping "how" separate from "what."
- **Traits:** adds a fairness argument — in a multinational workforce, trait labels invite bias because accent or manner can be mistaken for competence. *Removed:* KHALID's remark that traits could still inform development discussions.
- **Results:** names the two classic weaknesses in plain terms.
  - *Contamination* — things outside the employee's control (owner decisions, closed customer gates, road closures) affect the numbers.
  - *Deficiency* — a rate shows *what* happened, not *how*; a perfect on-time record can hide speeding. Gaming risk is also named (avoiding difficult jobs to protect a rate).
- **New "Why the mix differs" paragraph** explains the 60/40 versus 40/60 split. The manager controls more of the levers and has hundreds of trips behind each figure; each driver has a small sample and depends on dispatch and customers. It cites research showing objective and subjective measures agree only moderately (corrected r = .39, Bommer et al.), so measuring both beats choosing one.
- **Double counting:** expanded with a concrete example of how one breakdown can supply evidence for an outcome (M3b) and, separately, for the quality of the manager's reasoning (MB3).
- **Moved elsewhere:** the list of which objectives are scored went to Section 3; rater training went to Section 4.

### Section 3 — Results-based measures (758 → 862 words)

This section holds the most important technical changes in the report.

- **Trips and hire are split into separate sub-objectives:** M3 becomes M3a (trips) and M3b (hire); D3 becomes D3a and D3b.
- **Weighting by time spent replaces a simple 50/50 average.** KHALID averaged the trip score and the hire score equally, however much of the period each took. NAHAR weights each by its share of assigned working days. *Example:* a driver who spent 30 days on trips and 20 on hire has trips weighted 60% and hire 40%. This removes a contradiction in KHALID, which itself warned that lumping the two streams together "would conceal differences in exposure."
- **New minimum-sample rules.** KHALID said only that small samples need careful interpretation. NAHAR sets rules:
  - fewer than 10 commitments → no score, raw rate reported only;
  - 10 to 29 commitments → maximum score of 4;
  - 30 or more commitments → a 5 becomes possible.

  The reason: with 20 trips, a single miss moves the rate by five percentage points.
- **M1 (readiness):** now defines what a "complete readiness record" means — vehicle released, qualified driver assigned, transport documents issued, and owner approval where required.
- **M2 (breakdown response):** "two working hours" becomes "two hours". For a breakdown reported outside the agreed on-call window, the clock starts when the window opens.
- **M5 (cost control) gets two new conditions.**
  - All scheduled safety-critical maintenance must be done on time, so nobody can hit the budget by skipping maintenance. KHALID mentioned this risk in passing; NAHAR makes it a rule.
  - Any month above 100% of budget needs a written explanation approved by the owner.
- **M6 (coaching):** the follow-up deadline moves from **Day 90 to Day 85**. Day 90 is the last day of the period, which left no time to record evidence of the follow-up.
- **D5 (records):** clarified as one record per trip and one timesheet per hire day.
- **New evidence on management by objectives (MBO):** MBO programs raised productivity in 68 of 70 studies, with much larger gains when top management was committed (Rodgers & Hunter). The owner's visible participation is therefore made a precondition.
- **New "Risks and safeguards" paragraph** covers:
  - goals that push people toward shortcuts (Ordóñez et al.);
  - fatigue (Williamson & Friswell);
  - UK journey-planning guidance and International Labour Organization guidance, both used as design guidance, not Saudi law;
  - the Saudi Labor Law limit of **8 working hours a day or 48 a week** (article 98);
  - logging cancellations to stop people manipulating the denominator.

### Section 4 — Behavioral measures and evaluation method (769 → 785 words)

- **Competencies are coded in the text** (MB1–MB4, DB1–DB4), each with a described effective and ineffective behavior.
- **The in-between scale points are now defined.** KHALID said only that levels 2 and 4 are "intermediate patterns." NAHAR defines them:
  - *level 2* = usually meets expectations, with at least one documented clear failure;
  - *level 4* = consistently meets expectations, plus documented examples of stronger performance.
- **Evidence rules tightened:**
  - at least two dated observations per competency, otherwise the competency is marked "insufficient observation" and is **never** given a default 3;
  - a threshold competency rated below 3 triggers a development plan, whatever the overall score.
- **The "not a validated BARS" caveat now explains what is missing:** the retranslation step, in which independent judges re-sort incidents to check the anchors (Smith & Kendall).
- **New rating-bias paragraph:** research shows a rater's personal tendencies can explain more than half of the differences in ratings (Scullen et al.). Controls are listed, including the owner checking the manager's ten driver ratings for unsupported scores.
- **Moved here from Section 6:** the finding that more than a third of feedback interventions *reduced* performance (Kluger & DeNisi), used to argue that coaching should focus on the task, not the person.

### Section 5 — Appraisal forms, ratings and information sources (806 → 969 words)

- **Heading changed** (see the heading table above).
- **Each information source is now justified individually.** KHALID listed records plus customer and driver observations. NAHAR explains every source:
  - operating records come first;
  - Saudi electronic transport documents, mandatory since March 2023, can corroborate trip data;
  - customers give specific observations, not ratings;
  - drivers give upward comments on the manager, reported only if at least three respond, to protect anonymity;
  - peer ratings are excluded because drivers rarely see each other work;
  - both roles complete a self-assessment (Cawley et al.).
- **Removed:**
  - the point about the Transport General Authority's Bayan portal recording cargo and hazardous-material codes;
  - the caveat that telematics and full historical records are not assumed.
- **Appraisal period extended:** quarterly developmental reviews continue after the pilot, and any annual administrative summary waits until two cycles have tested the targets.
- **A clear rule now turns the decimal score into a final rating.** KHALID said decimal scores would be kept "without automatic rounding into a final category", yet still awarded final ratings of 3 and 4 with no rule linking them. NAHAR adds:
  - fixed cutoffs: 1.00–1.49 = 1; 1.50–2.49 = 2; 2.50–3.49 = 3; 3.50–4.49 = 4; 4.50–5.00 = 5;
  - the rater may move the final rating by **at most one category**, with written reasons;
  - a bigger move needs second-level review — by the owner for drivers, and by the agreed neutral reviewer for the manager, because no one sits above the owner.
- **New requirement "gates":**
  - a controllable unmet requirement caps the final rating at **3**, unless the owner documents an exception;
  - a **safety** failure (unmet D1 or D2, or deploying a truck with an open release-blocking defect under M1) caps the final rating at **2**, with no exception allowed.
- **Kept from KHALID:** the rule that genuinely not-applicable work can be reweighted, while missing evidence must be investigated and never replaced by a default score.
- **Dispute route tightened:** a 10-working-day window to appeal, and a driver still unhappy after owner review may go to the neutral reviewer.
- **Moved to Section 6:** the Cerasoli et al. point that incentives predict quantity more than quality.

### Section 6 — Critical evaluation and recommendations (295 → 344 words)

- **KHALID** discussed two challenges (fair attribution; acceptance and paperwork) and ended with one paragraph recommending a pilot.
- **NAHAR** states strengths and limitations directly and **puts a number on the workload:** about 40 manager-hours a quarter, roughly 6% of the manager's working time, once meetings, spot audits and logged observations are included.
- **Seven numbered recommendations replace the single paragraph:**
  1. run a 90-day pilot with no pay or disciplinary consequences;
  2. train raters on shared cases and hold an anchor workshop;
  3. recalibrate targets after the pilot, never retroactively;
  4. use existing documents rather than new paperwork;
  5. build acceptance through participation;
  6. publish the dispute route in advance;
  7. keep development and pay decisions separate until ratings prove stable.
- **Removed:** two cautions from KHALID —
  - agreement between raters alone cannot prove the measures fit this company's work;
  - scores are structured summaries of evidence, not precise estimates of personal contribution.
